A concept album is a musical album whose tracks hold a larger purpose or meaning collectively than they do individually. This is typically achieved through a single central narrative or theme, which can be instrumental, compositional, or lyrical. Roy Shuker defines concept albums and rock operas as albums that are 'unified by a theme, which can be instrumental, compositional, narrative, or lyrical... In this form, the album changed from a collection of heterogeneous songs into a narrative work with a single theme, in which individual songs segue into one another.'

According to author Edward Macan, concept albums as a recurrent theme in progressive rock were directly inspired by the counterculture: 'the consistent use of lengthy forms such as the programmatic song cycle of the concept album and the multimovement suite underscores the hippies' new, drug-induced conception of time.' Popular consensus for the first rock concept album favours the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967). Canonical progressive-rock examples include Genesis's The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway (1974), Pink Floyd's The Wall (1979) and The Dark Side of the Moon, and Yes's Close to the Edge and Tales from Topographic Oceans - the latter a double concept album. Since 2019, the classical music magazine Gramophone has included a 'concept album' category in its annual awards.
