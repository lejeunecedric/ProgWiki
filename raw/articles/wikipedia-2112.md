2112 (pronounced "twenty-one twelve") is the fourth studio album by Canadian rock band Rush, released in March 1976 by Mercury Records. Following the disappointing sales of their previous albums Caress of Steel and Fly by Night, 2112 became the band's first major commercial success. The album reached No. 5 on the Canadian charts and No. 61 in the United States.

The band was in financial hardship due to the disappointing sales of 1975's Caress of Steel. Mercury, their international label, considered dropping them but granted one more album following negotiations with manager Ray Danniels. Despite pressure to deliver more commercial material, the band decided to continue developing its progressive rock direction and made the 20-minute futuristic science-fiction title track occupy side one of 2112, with a collection of shorter songs on side two that display their hard rock roots.

Side one is occupied by the 20-minute song "2112", a seven-part track based on a story by drummer and lyricist Neil Peart, who credited Russian-American novelist Ayn Rand (author of the dystopian novella Anthem). The story is set in the city of Megadon in the year 2112, after an intergalactic war forces many planets to be ruled by the Solar Federation, where individualism and creativity are outlawed. A cabal of priests in the temples of Syrinx control the population. An unnamed protagonist finds a guitar in a cave and rediscovers the lost art of music, but the priests destroy it. The song ends with the ambiguous spoken line: "Attention all planets of the Solar Federation: We have assumed control."

The seven parts are: I. Overture, II. The Temples of Syrinx, III. Discovery, IV. Presentation, V. Oracle: The Dream, VI. Soliloquy, VII. Grand Finale. The "Overture" begins with a soundscape from musician and album cover artist Hugh Syme performed on his ARP Odyssey synthesizer, and borrows a short sequence from Tchaikovsky's 1812 Overture.

For the recording, Lee used a Rickenbacker 4001 bass with stereo output; producer Terry Brown fed one pickup into the mixing board and into a compressor, and the other was channelled into Lee's Electro-Voice speakers. Lifeson played a 1968 Gibson ES-335 for most electric parts, with some leads on a Gibson Les Paul Standard.

2112 was released to favourable reviews and quickly outsold the band's previous albums. It remains the band's second-highest-selling album behind Moving Pictures, with more than 4 million copies sold in the US alone. It is listed in the book 1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die and ranked second on Rolling Stone's readers' poll of favourite prog rock albums of all time.
