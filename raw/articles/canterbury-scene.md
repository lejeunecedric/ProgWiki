The Canterbury scene (Canterbury sound) is a musical scene originating in the city of Canterbury, Kent, England during the late 1960s and early 1970s, associated with progressive rock. It describes a loosely defined, improvisational style blending elements of jazz, rock, and psychedelia. The scene was born from the ashes of The Wilde Flowers, a psych-pop outfit active in the mid to late 60s; after the band broke up in 1969 its members went on to play in numerous bands (Soft Machine, Caravan, Gong, Hatfield and the North, Egg, and National Health) with ever-changing overlapping personnel. Musicians include Hugh Hopper, Steve Hillage, Dave Stewart, Robert Wyatt, Kevin Ayers, Richard Sinclair, Daevid Allen, and Mike Ratledge.

Didier Malherbe (of Gong) defined the scene as having "certain chord changes, in particular the use of minor second chords, certain harmonic combinations, and a great clarity in the aesthetics, and a way of improvising that is very different from what is done in jazz." Robert Wyatt disliked the label: "It's an artificial label, a journalistic thing... it just happened." Hugh Hopper, who lived in nearby Whitstable, called it "rather artificial... a journalistic thing." The Canterbury scene's music is marked by whimsicality, touches of psychedelia, abstruse lyrics knotted with jazz-derived improvisation, "the tension between complicated harmonies, extended improvisations, and the sincere desire to write catchy pop songs."
