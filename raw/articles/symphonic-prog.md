Symphonic rock (also called symphonic prog or symphonic progressive rock) is a subgenre of progressive rock that attempts to adopt the scope, structure, and tonal palette of Western classical music while retaining a rock band's instrumentation and power. Symphonic rock is often used as a synonym for progressive rock itself (Wikipedia lists "symphonic rock" among the other names for progressive rock). It is characterised by long, multi-movement suites; recurring leitmotifs; dramatic dynamic shifts; and richly layered keyboard textures (Mellotron, Hammond organ, Minimoog). Arrangements often imitate orchestral writing through counterpoint, thematic development, and sectional contrast, with guitars and keyboards sharing lead roles much like orchestral sections.

Progressive rock groups such as Yes and Genesis experienced great worldwide success in the early to mid-1970s; in the late 1970s it declined. The genre reached maturity by the early 1970s with ambitious concept albums and side-long compositions by groups like Yes, Genesis, Emerson, Lake & Palmer, and Renaissance. Few artists incorporated literal classical themes to any significant degree; direct emulators of classical music were a minority (ELP, Renaissance, Ekseption). Core instrumentation: drums, electric bass, electric and acoustic guitars, Mellotron/choir and string pads, Hammond organ, Moog/Minimoog, piano, sometimes chamber wind instruments.

Distinct bands in the symphonic strand (contrast with the more austere King Crimson): the accessible, melodic "classic prog" of the early 1970s carried by Yes, Genesis, ELP and Camel. British symphonic bands achieved great success in the 1970s, and the genre fragmented after the late 1970s; neo-prog in the 1980s and Scandinavian/American acts (The Flower Kings, Spock's Beard, Transatlantic) revived symphonic values in the 1990s.
