# Progressive Rock

Progressive rock (shortened to **prog rock** or simply **prog**) is a broad genre of rock music that primarily developed in the United States and United Kingdom through the mid- to late 1960s. Initially termed "progressive pop", it emerged from psychedelic bands who abandoned standard pop or rock traditions in favour of instrumental and compositional techniques more commonly associated with jazz, folk, or classical music, while keeping rock's instrumentation.^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]

## What makes it "progressive"
- **Lyrics** became more poetic; **technology** was harnessed for new sounds; music approached the condition of "art"; and the **studio, rather than the stage, became the focus** of musical activity — often music for listening rather than dancing.^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]
- The **album format overtook the single**, enabling long-form statements.
- It is associated with long solos, extended pieces, fantastic lyrics, grandiose stage sets and costumes, and an obsessive dedication to technical skill and [[virtuosity|virtuosity]].^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]
- It is a fusion of styles, approaches and genres — eclectic by design, mixing high and low culture. Only a minority of groups ([[emerson-lake-palmer|Emerson, Lake & Palmer]], Renaissance, Ekseption) directly emulated classical music; most absorbed it more loosely.^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]

## History and decline
In the early to mid-1970s, groups such as [[pink-floyd|Pink Floyd]] and [[yes|Yes]] achieved great worldwide success. In the late 1970s the genre declined and never fully recovered; critics often dismissed it as "pretentious", "pompous" and "overblown". After that it fragmented into numerous forms — some bands kept commercial success into the 1980s with changed lineups and more compact song structures.^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]

## The family tree
- Early groups with progressive features are retroactively called **proto-prog**.
- The [[canterbury-scene|Canterbury scene]] (late 1960s) emphasised wind instruments, complex chord changes and long improvisations.
- [[rock-in-opposition|Rock in Opposition]] (late 1970s) was more avant-garde; combined with Canterbury it created **avant-prog**.
- In the 1980s, [[neo-prog|neo-prog]] enjoyed some commercial success, though it was accused of being derivative.
- [[symphonic-prog|Symphonic prog]] is the orchestral "classic prog" mainstream; [[krautrock|krautrock]], [[space-rock|space rock]], [[zeuhl|zeuhl]] and [[progressive-metal|progressive metal]] are further branches.
