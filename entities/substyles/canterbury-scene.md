# Canterbury Scene

The **Canterbury scene** (or **Canterbury sound**, "Canterbury music") is a musical scene originating in the city of Canterbury, Kent, England during the late 1960s and early 1970s, associated with [[progressive-rock|progressive rock]]. It describes a loosely defined, improvisational style blending elements of jazz, rock, and psychedelia. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Origins

The scene was born from the ashes of **The Wilde Flowers**, a psych-pop outfit active in the mid-to-late 60s. After the band split up in 1969, its members went on to play in numerous bands — Soft Machine, Caravan, [[gong|Gong]], Hatfield and the North, Egg, National Health — with ever-changing, overlapping personnel. Its participants include Robert Wyatt, Kevin Ayers, Hugh Hopper, Mike Ratledge, Richard Sinclair, Daevid Allen, Steve Hillage and Dave Stewart. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## The "Canterbury sound"

The scene is marked by whimsicality, touches of psychedelia, rather abstruse lyrics, and improvisation derived from jazz. Didier Malherbe (of Gong) defined it as "certain chord changes, in particular the use of minor second chords, certain harmonic combinations, and a great clarity in the aesthetics, and a way of improvising that is very different from what is done in jazz." Robert Wyatt disliked the label: "It's an artificial label, a journalistic thing... it just happened... I didn't even know it meant me." Hugh Hopper, who lived near Whitstable, said "I think it's rather artificial, a journalistic thing." ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Soft Machine lineage

The scene's central thread runs through Soft Machine, formed when Robert Wyatt, Daevid Allen, Kevin Ayers and Mike Ratledge left Canterbury for London. Later incarnations of Soft Machine moved increasingly toward jazz-rock, and Canterbury musicians fed the jazz-fusion scene heavily (Hugh Hopper into touring with Soft Machine and beyond; Robert Wyatt into solo work including *Rock Bottom*). ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Legacy

The Canterbury scene, combined with [[rock-in-opposition|Rock in Opposition]], created the avant-prog strand. Its jazz-inflected, quirky, improvisational identity is often played off against the orchestral [[symphonic-prog|symphonic prog]] of Yes/Genesis/ELP.
