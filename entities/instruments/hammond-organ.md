# Hammond Organ

The **Hammond organ** is an electric organ invented by Laurens Hammond and John M. Hanert, first manufactured in 1935. Until 1975 its sound was generated by rotating a metal **tonewheel** near an electromagnetic pickup, amplified into a speaker cabinet — making it, strictly speaking, an electromechanical rather than electronic instrument. It is commonly played through a rotating **Leslie speaker**, whose Doppler pitch shifts give the instrument its characteristic swirling tone.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]

## How it works
- **Drawbars** vary the sound: nine sliders per manual mix the fundamental and its harmonics, making the Hammond a form of additive synthesis. The setting 888000000 (drawbars 16′, 5+1⁄3′ and 8′ out) is the "classic" Jimmy Smith sound.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]
- **B-3 and C-3** console models (introduced 1954) added "harmonic percussion" and stayed in continuous production through early 1975; they are the definitive pro models. The **L-100** was a cheaper economy spinet that sold particularly well in the UK.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]
- Key click and tonewheel crosstalk were originally design defects, but players liked them and they became part of the signature sound that modern digital clones explicitly emulate.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]

## Role in progressive rock
The Hammond was a key instrument in progressive rock. Author Edward Macan credits its versatility — it can play both chords and lead lines, and range from quiet and clean to what [[keith-emerson|Keith Emerson]] called a "tacky, aggressive, almost distorted, angry sound".^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]

- **[[keith-emerson|Keith Emerson]]** is the archetypal prog Hammondist. He discovered the instrument after hearing jazz organist Jack McDuff's "Rock Candy", and with the Nice used and abused an L-100 — putting knives in it, setting fire to it, playing it upside down, and riding it across stage — before continuing that theatrical abuse alongside other keyboards in [[emerson-lake-palmer|Emerson, Lake & Palmer]]. He preferred the C-3's sound as "far superior" to the cheaper L-100.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]^[raw/articles/keith-emerson.md]
- **Jon Lord** of Deep Purple modified his Hammond to be played through a Marshall stack, producing a growling, overdriven sound that became his trademark — the rock-organ lineage Emerson built on.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]
- Other prominent prog Hammondists include Yes's Tony Kaye and [[rick-wakeman|Rick Wakeman]], Genesis's Tony Banks, Pink Floyd's Richard Wright, and Van der Graaf Generator's Hugh Banton (who rewired his E-100 for "unimaginable sonic chaos").^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]

## Legacy
The original company abandoned tonewheels for integrated circuits in the 1970s and went out of business in 1985; the name was bought by Suzuki, which launched the digital "New B-3" in 2002. Lighter portable emulations ("clonewheel organs") from Korg, Roland and Clavia keep the tonewheel sound available on stage.^[raw/articles/hammond-organ.md]
