# What is the Canterbury scene?

## Answer

The **Canterbury scene** (or Canterbury sound) is a musical scene that originated in the city of Canterbury, Kent, England during the late 1960s and early 1970s. Associated with progressive rock, it describes a loosely defined, improvisational style blending elements of jazz, rock and psychedelia. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Part 1 — Origins

The scene was born from the ashes of The Wilde Flowers, a psych-pop outfit active in the mid-to-late 60s. After the band broke up in 1969, its members went on to play in numerous bands — Soft Machine, Caravan, Gong, Hatfield and the North, Egg and National Health — with ever-changing, overlapping personnel. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Part 2 — The sound

The scene is defined less by a single sound than by a network of musicians. Common elements include jazz-derived improvisation, complex chord changes, wind instruments, whimsicality, touches of psychedelia and abstruse lyrics. Didier Malherbe (of Gong) defined it by "certain chord changes, in particular the use of minor second chords, certain harmonic combinations, and a great clarity in the aesthetics, and a way of improvising that is very different from what is done in jazz." ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Part 3 — The soft machine lineage

The scene's central lineage runs through Soft Machine, formed when Robert Wyatt and Daevid Allen left Canterbury for London. The band's early albums blended psychedelic pop with jazz; later incarnations moved toward instrumental jazz-rock. The overlapping personnel — Hopper, Ratledge, Wyatt, Ayers — define the "Canterbury" identity more than any single sound. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Part 4 — A contested label

The term is partly a journalistic construct. Robert Wyatt disliked it, noting he was born elsewhere and merely went to school in Canterbury; Hugh Hopper called it "rather artificial... a journalistic thing." The scene is defined more by a network of musicians than by geography. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Part 5 — Legacy

The Canterbury style, combined with [[rock-in-opposition|Rock in Opposition]], helped create avant-prog. It stands as the jazz-rooted, quirky counterpoint to the orchestral [[symphonic-prog|symphonic prog]] mainstream. ^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]

## Synthesis

The Canterbury scene is best understood not as a genre with a fixed sound but as a family of musicians from the Canterbury area who shared a jazz-inflected, whimsical, improvisational approach to progressive rock. Its influence runs through avant-prog and beyond, and it remains one of the two great British poles of early-70s prog alongside symphonic prog. ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]
