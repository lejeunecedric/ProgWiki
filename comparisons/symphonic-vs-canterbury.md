# Symphonic Prog vs the Canterbury Scene

Two British poles of the early 1970s: the orchestral, virtuoso [[symphonic-prog|symphonic prog]] mainstream versus the jazz-rooted, quirky [[canterbury-scene|Canterbury scene]]. Both grew out of the same first wave of progressive rock, but they diverged sharply in sound, attitude and lineage.

## The symphonic pole

Symphonic prog adopts the scope, structure and tonal palette of Western classical music while keeping a rock band's instrumentation and power. It is characterised by long multi-movement suites, recurring leitmotifs, dramatic dynamic shifts and richly layered keyboard textures. It reached maturity in the early 1970s with ambitious concept albums by Yes, Genesis, ELP and Camel. ^[raw/articles/symphonic-prog.md]

## The Canterbury pole

The Canterbury scene describes a loosely defined, improvisational style blending jazz, rock and psychedelia, born from the ashes of The Wilde Flowers. Its members went on to play in Soft Machine, Caravan, Gong, Hatfield and the North, Egg and National Health, with ever-changing overlapping personnel. Didier Malherbe defined it by "certain chord changes, in particular the use of minor second chords... and a way of improvising that is very different from what is done in jazz." ^[raw/articles/canterbury-scene.md]

## Verdict

The two poles represent opposite answers to the same question — how to make rock "progressive." Symphonic prog looked to the concert hall for grandeur and structure; the Canterbury scene looked to the jazz club for looseness and wit. Symphonic prog dominated the charts and the arena; Canterbury remained a cult, improvisational network. Together they bracket the range of classic British prog, and their combination (with [[rock-in-opposition|RIO]]) produced avant-prog. ^[raw/articles/progressive-rock.md]
